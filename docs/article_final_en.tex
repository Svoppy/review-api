\begin{abstract}
This paper presents an empirical study of joint review sentiment analysis and review authenticity detection in e-commerce. The problem is practically important because online retail platforms depend not only on identifying whether customer feedback is positive or negative, but also on determining whether that feedback is trustworthy. A highly accurate sentiment model may still produce analytically misleading business signals if a substantial share of input reviews is deceptive, manipulated, or automatically generated.

To address this limitation, the paper proposes a multitask Transformer-based approach with a shared encoder and two task-specific classification heads: one for sentiment classification and one for authenticity detection. The central methodological contribution is not multitask learning in itself, but a joint formulation designed for partial supervision across heterogeneous public corpora, where no single dataset provides both labels at useful scale. The executed study uses locally prepared subsets of \texttt{RuReviews} and \texttt{MAiDE-up}, while the repository preserves a broader extension path through \texttt{Perekrestok Reviews}, \texttt{OpSpam}, and \texttt{FraudDataset (Yelp)}.

Beyond the modeling component, the study develops a reproducibility-oriented end-to-end system, \texttt{ReviewGuard}, that covers data normalization, baseline training, single-task Transformer baselines, multitask training, model export, and API-based inference. The service also includes a web interface for interactive review analysis, but this systems layer is treated as supporting infrastructure rather than as a stand-alone scientific result. On a fixed controlled low-resource split of 2{,}000 reviews with three train seeds, the comparison package shows asymmetric transfer: the multitask model significantly improves authenticity detection on the current study protocol, while sentiment remains statistically tied with the sentiment-only Transformer and clearly below the classical baseline. The resulting claim is therefore intentionally bounded: under a reproducible mixed-source low-resource setting, multitask transfer appears beneficial for authenticity but is not yet supported as a sentiment improvement.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} sentiment analysis, review authenticity detection, fake review detection, multitask learning, multilingual Transformer, e-commerce, web system.

\section{Introduction}

User reviews are among the most influential sources of information in digital commerce. They affect product ranking, merchant reputation, customer trust, and purchase decisions. For this reason, automatic review analysis has become a standard natural language processing application in e-commerce. Most existing systems focus on sentiment analysis, that is, identifying whether a review expresses a positive, neutral, or negative opinion.

However, real-world e-commerce review analysis cannot rely on sentiment alone. Platforms increasingly face deceptive, incentivized, spam-like, or automatically generated reviews. When such reviews are not filtered, the resulting sentiment signal may be technically correct for the text itself but analytically misleading for the underlying business reality. In other words, a system that detects polarity but ignores authenticity may still support the wrong decision-making process.

This observation suggests that review sentiment and review authenticity are related tasks rather than isolated ones. Both operate on the same text input, both depend on lexical, semantic, and stylistic signals, and both affect downstream trust and analytics. Despite this connection, most deployed systems still train separate models for sentiment analysis and authenticity detection. This separation is understandable from a practical standpoint, but it increases inference cost, duplicates system complexity, and does not exploit potential positive transfer between related tasks.

The goal of this study is to develop a multitask Transformer-based model and a web system for joint sentiment analysis and review authenticity detection in e-commerce. The work combines a research contribution and an engineering contribution. On the research side, it investigates whether a shared Transformer encoder with task-specific heads can improve or stabilize performance across both tasks. On the engineering side, it proposes a reproducible data-to-service pipeline that connects dataset normalization, model training, checkpoint export, API inference, and a browser-based user interface. Importantly, the present article reports a bounded empirical study rather than a benchmark-wide leaderboard claim: it tests a concrete low-resource mixed-source setting, quantifies uncertainty, and draws conclusions only within that protocol. The system is therefore positioned as decision-support infrastructure for moderation and analysis rather than as a substitute for dataset-specific interpretation or methodological judgment.

The remainder of the article is organized as follows. First, the study is positioned in the literatures on multitask NLP, review authenticity detection, and multilingual review sentiment analysis. Next, the paper formalizes the joint task, describes the heterogeneous data regime and the multitask training design, and presents the current system architecture and executed study results. Finally, it discusses the evidential scope of the findings, their threats to validity, and the most important extensions for broader evaluation.

\section{Related Work}

The theoretical basis of this study lies in modern multitask learning for NLP. Classical work showed that joint learning of related tasks may improve generalization through shared inductive bias \cite{caruana1997}. Later Transformer-based approaches such as MT-DNN demonstrated that a shared encoder with task-specific heads can remain effective across multiple language-understanding tasks \cite{liu2019}. Recent surveys emphasize that positive transfer depends on task relatedness, supervision density, and parameter-sharing strategy rather than arising automatically from parameter sharing alone \cite{zhang2023}.

Within review authenticity detection, multitask and cross-domain learning are especially relevant because labeled deception data are fragmented across platforms, languages, and manipulation types. Prior work on deceptive review spam detection established the classical truthful-versus-deceptive benchmark setting \cite{ott2011}, while Hai et al. showed that exploiting task relatedness and unlabeled data can improve deceptive review detection across domains \cite{hai2016}. Subsequent work made clear that authenticity should not be treated as a single closed-domain phenomenon: related studies highlighted hijacked reviews \cite{daryani2021}, platform-specific moderation settings \cite{nayak2022}, and the broader operational demands of deployment-oriented trust systems.

The emergence of large language models introduced a new authenticity threat model: fluent AI-generated reviews. Recent studies showed that LLM-generated reviews form a distinct detection challenge rather than a trivial extension of earlier deceptive-review settings \cite{liyanage2024}. This line was strengthened by multilingual datasets such as \texttt{MAiDE-up}, which demonstrated that detection difficulty varies across languages, domains, and sentiment profiles \cite{ignat2025}. Few-shot and multi-domain evidence from \texttt{DetectAIRev} further suggests that cross-generator and cross-domain robustness remains unresolved \cite{agrahari2025}.

On the sentiment side, e-commerce review analysis benefits from realistic large-scale corpora rather than only narrow benchmark datasets. Large review resources such as \texttt{Amazon Reviews 2023} and \texttt{MARC} illustrate the value of naturalistic review text at scale \cite{hou2023,keung2020}. For Russian-language experimentation, \texttt{RuReviews} and \texttt{Perekrestok Reviews} provide a practical combination of direct sentiment labels and large in-domain review text \cite{sismetanin,perekrestok}.

Taken together, the literature reveals a more specific gap than a generic lack of multitask modeling. MT-DNN validates the shared-encoder paradigm, but on fully supervised NLU benchmarks rather than partially labeled heterogeneous review corpora \cite{liu2019}. Hai et al. demonstrate task-related transfer within deceptive review detection, but do not formulate joint sentiment and authenticity prediction as one review-level learning problem \cite{hai2016}. Nayak and Garera show the value of unified moderation models in production, but their setting is platform-specific moderation rather than an explicitly reproducible scientific study of sentiment-plus-authenticity under asymmetric supervision \cite{nayak2022}. The resulting open problem is therefore joint sentiment and authenticity modeling under partial supervision, heterogeneous benchmark semantics, and bounded empirical reporting.

\section{Problem Definition and Research Hypotheses}

Let a review text be denoted by $x$. The system must predict two related targets:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
  \item $y_s$, the sentiment class, where $y_s \in \{\text{negative}, \text{neutral}, \text{positive}\}$;
  \item $y_a$, the authenticity class, where $y_a \in \{\text{authentic}, \text{fake}\}$.
\end{enumerate}

An important challenge is that publicly available datasets rarely provide both labels for the same review at scale. As a result, the learning setup must support partially labeled records, where a review may be annotated for only one of the two tasks.

The main research hypothesis is that joint learning with a shared Transformer encoder will:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5cm]
  \item preserve competitive performance for sentiment classification relative to a single-task Transformer baseline;
  \item improve or stabilize authenticity detection through richer shared language representations;
  \item produce a more compact and practically deployable inference system than two independent models.
\end{itemize}

The study evaluates the following specific hypotheses:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5cm]
  \item \textbf{H1}: the multitask model does not underperform the single-task Transformer on sentiment analysis;
  \item \textbf{H2}: the multitask model matches or outperforms text-only baselines for authenticity detection;
  \item \textbf{H3}: joint training improves robustness only if it improves at least two of the following slice-based indicators on the same protocol: mean source-wise Macro-F1, worst-slice Macro-F1, and the best-minus-worst robustness gap.
\end{itemize}

The present study tests H1--H3 on a matched low-resource mixed-source protocol with baseline, single-task, and multitask comparisons on identical splits. Because the executed dataset bundle is narrower than the full repository data plan, any supported hypothesis is interpreted within this bounded study scope rather than as a universal benchmark claim.

\subsection{Operational Definition of Authenticity}

In this study, the term \textit{authenticity} is used operationally rather than philosophically. A review is treated as \texttt{authentic} when it belongs to the truthful or non-manipulated side of a given benchmark, and as \texttt{fake} when it belongs to the deceptive, fraud-associated, or AI-generated side of that benchmark. This operationalization is practical for supervised learning, but it should not be mistaken for a claim that all negative examples reflect the same real-world phenomenon. One benchmark may represent controlled human deception, another may reflect silver fraud signals, and another may represent synthetic generation by language models. The binary label is therefore an engineering abstraction that enables joint training, while the final interpretation of results must remain dataset-specific.

\section{Research Contribution}

The novelty of this study does not lie in introducing multitask learning as such, since shared-encoder architectures are already well established in NLP and have been exemplified by systems such as MT-DNN \cite{liu2019}. Nor does it lie simply in deploying unified moderation models for reviews, which has already been explored in platform-specific industrial settings \cite{nayak2022}, or in exploiting task relatedness within deceptive review detection, which earlier work had already studied in narrower authenticity formulations \cite{hai2016}. The literature gap addressed here is more specific: prior work does not adequately study sentiment analysis and review authenticity detection as a single review-level learning problem under realistic partial supervision, where labels are distributed asymmetrically across heterogeneous public corpora and authenticity itself spans non-identical benchmark semantics.

This paper therefore contributes a joint formulation of sentiment and authenticity prediction designed explicitly for partially labeled, mixed-source review data. Its scientific contribution is the combination of three elements: first, a unified multitask setup in which sentiment and authenticity are learned together even when individual examples carry only one of the two labels; second, an explicit operational treatment of authenticity that allows deceptive, fraud-associated, and AI-generated review labels to be incorporated into one training framework without claiming that these phenomena are empirically interchangeable; and third, a fully reproducible end-to-end pipeline that makes this task pair executable from data normalization through training, export, and inference. Importantly, the present article evaluates this formulation only within the completed pilot study and does not present the broader planned benchmark as if it were already experimentally established.

This framing matters for two reasons. Methodologically, it offers a responsible alternative to overgeneralized benchmark claims by treating the current results as bounded empirical evidence tied to one controlled low-resource protocol rather than as proof of universal multitask superiority. Substantively, it turns a practically important but usually fragmented moderation problem into a transparent research object: not just whether shared training can help, but under what supervision and data conditions it helps one task more than the other. In this sense, the paper's main contribution is a reproducible and scientifically circumscribed foundation for studying joint trust-aware review analysis, together with pilot evidence that such joint learning can yield meaningful authenticity gains even when sentiment gains are not yet demonstrated.

This explicit scope framing is not merely a conservative disclaimer. It is methodologically necessary because the scientific contribution of the article depends on comparative empirical evidence that is real and auditable, yet still conditional on the executed data bundle and protocol.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Multitask Model Architecture}

The proposed model uses hard parameter sharing. The architecture is a shared multilingual Transformer encoder with two task-specific heads. The repository also supports larger backbones such as \texttt{XLM-RoBERTa}, but the executed study reported here uses \texttt{distilbert-base-multilingual-cased} because it permits repeated CPU-side runs on the current low-resource protocol. The shared encoder produces a contextual representation for each review, which is then passed to two independent classification heads:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5cm]
  \item $\text{Head}_s$ for sentiment classification;
  \item $\text{Head}_a$ for authenticity detection.
\end{itemize}

Formally,
\begin{align}
h &= \text{Encoder}(x), \\
p_s &= \text{softmax}(W_s h + b_s), \\
p_a &= \text{softmax}(W_a h + b_a).
\end{align}

The training loss is defined as a weighted sum:
\begin{equation}
L = \lambda_s L_{\text{sentiment}} + \lambda_a L_{\text{authenticity}},
\end{equation}
where each task-specific term is included only when the corresponding label exists. This makes the approach compatible with partial supervision.

In practical terms, this means that a mini-batch with only sentiment labels updates the shared encoder and the sentiment head but contributes no authenticity loss, while a mini-batch with only authenticity labels behaves symmetrically. In the current study, this partial-supervision mechanism is not hypothetical: it is the central reason a mixed-source corpus can be used for one matched experiment despite asymmetric label availability.

\subsection{Baselines}

To evaluate the actual contribution of multitask learning, the following comparison lines are included:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
  \item \texttt{TF-IDF + Logistic Regression} for each task;
  \item \texttt{Single-task Transformer} for sentiment classification;
  \item \texttt{Single-task Transformer} for authenticity detection;
  \item \texttt{Multitask Transformer} with a shared encoder.
\end{enumerate}

These models are compared on identical splits wherever task coverage permits. Reporting a multitask result on one sample and a single-task result on another would not be methodologically acceptable, so the article keeps the comparison bundle tied to one fixed prepared input and one fixed split policy.

\subsection{Unified Data Schema}

The project uses a unified record format with the following fields:
\texttt{text}, \texttt{sentiment\_label}, \texttt{authenticity\_label}, \texttt{source}, \texttt{language}, \texttt{domain}, and \texttt{metadata}. This design makes heterogeneous datasets interoperable and supports reproducible experimentation across tasks and domains.

\subsection{Training Protocol}

Experiments are repeated across three random seeds, with mean and standard deviation reported for the main Transformer metrics. Train, validation, and test splits are leakage-safe at the prepared-record level: duplicate or near-duplicate records must not cross split boundaries, and source-specific partitions remain grouped where required by dataset design.

For partially labeled training, each mini-batch contributes only the loss terms supported by available labels. Because dataset sizes differ across sources, the repository now supports balancing controls such as class weighting and source-balanced sampling, but the executed study reported here keeps one matched protocol for all compared models so that relative transfer effects can be observed cleanly.

The executed study uses \texttt{distilbert-base-multilingual-cased} with \texttt{max\_length=128}, \texttt{batch\_size=8}, \texttt{learning\_rate=2e-5}, \texttt{weight\_decay=0.01}, \texttt{epochs=1}, fixed \texttt{split\_seed=42}, train seeds $\{11, 21, 42\}$, and CPU execution. This is a compute-constrained protocol rather than a fully optimized training regime, so the article interprets its results as evidence about comparative transfer behavior under matched conditions rather than as a leaderboard-style upper bound. The repository also includes a stronger extension protocol with \texttt{epochs=4}, balanced class weighting, source-balanced sampling, early stopping, and split-level audit for follow-up experiments.

\section{Web System Architecture}

The proposed system is not only a research model but also a deployable application. The current \texttt{ReviewGuard} architecture includes:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
  \item raw-data normalization and merging utilities;
  \item a training CLI for classical baselines, single-task Transformers, and multitask Transformers;
  \item checkpoint export logic;
  \item a \texttt{FastAPI} backend;
  \item a lightweight browser-based interface.
\end{enumerate}

The inference endpoint \texttt{/analyze} is designed to return:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5cm]
  \item predicted sentiment label;
  \item sentiment confidence score;
  \item predicted authenticity label;
  \item authenticity confidence score;
  \item an explanation object.
\end{itemize}

The explanation layer is intentionally lightweight and practical. It exposes ranked task probabilities, token-count information, truncation status, margin-based risk flags, and a compact provenance summary of the training corpus used for the exported checkpoint. It should therefore be treated as a transparency aid rather than a fully causal interpretability framework, and in the article it should remain outside the core scientific contribution claims. In a submission-ready version, this material should appear in the system section or appendix rather than in the central hypothesis framing.

\section{Experimental Design}

The executed evaluation bundle in this article is intentionally controlled:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
  \item a deterministic sample of 1{,}000 \texttt{RuReviews} records for sentiment supervision;
  \item a deterministic sample of 1{,}000 \texttt{MAiDE-up} records for authenticity supervision and multilingual stress;
  \item one merged partially labeled corpus of 2{,}000 reviews;
  \item four model families on the same split: \texttt{TF-IDF + Logistic Regression}, \texttt{single-task Transformer} for sentiment, \texttt{single-task Transformer} for authenticity, and \texttt{multitask Transformer};
  \item repeated Transformer runs across train seeds $\{11,21,42\}$ while keeping the split fixed.
\end{enumerate}

The merged corpus is split 80/10/10 into train, validation, and test partitions using a deterministic split seed of 42. This yields 200 sentiment test examples and 100 authenticity-labeled test examples. The main evaluation metrics are \texttt{Accuracy}, \texttt{Macro-F1}, \texttt{Weighted-F1}, macro-averaged precision, macro-averaged recall, and the confusion matrix. For authenticity detection, \texttt{Macro-F1} is particularly important in the presence of class imbalance.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

To reduce overinterpretation of small metric differences, Transformer results are summarized not only as point estimates but also as repeated-run and paired-comparison statistics:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5cm]
  \item mean $\pm$ standard deviation across train seeds for Transformer models;
  \item Student-$t$ 95\% confidence intervals across seeds for model-level metrics;
  \item paired bootstrap confidence intervals over the fixed test split for primary model deltas;
  \item approximate randomization tests for the same paired contrasts.
\end{itemize}

This layer is intentionally modest. It is designed to support comparative reasoning under one controlled protocol, not to simulate a fully confirmatory multi-benchmark statistical program.

\subsection{Robustness Hypothesis Boundary}

For \textbf{H3}, aggregate metrics alone are not sufficient. Robustness is operationalized through slice-based reporting, at minimum:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5cm]
  \item mean Macro-F1 across source slices;
  \item worst-slice Macro-F1;
  \item the best-minus-worst robustness gap;
  \item where feasible, a leave-one-source-out performance delta.
\end{itemize}

Because the executed study uses one authenticity benchmark family and one main sentiment source, H3 is only partially testable in the current article. It remains a pre-specified robustness criterion rather than a fully resolved claim.

The resulting joint set contains 2{,}000 reviews, all labeled for sentiment and 1{,}000 labeled for authenticity.

The strongest result is authenticity detection. Repeated runs across three train seeds show that multitask authenticity reaches macro-F1 $0.9160 \pm 0.0524$, compared with $0.8328 \pm 0.1075$ for the authenticity-only Transformer and $0.7999$ for the baseline. The multitask-versus-single-task authenticity macro-F1 delta is $+0.0832$ with a 95\% bootstrap confidence interval of $[0.0447, 0.1274]$ and approximate-randomization $p=0.0005$.

Sentiment behaves differently. Multitask macro-F1 is $0.5856 \pm 0.0665$ versus $0.5814 \pm 0.0526$ for the sentiment-only Transformer and $0.7368$ for the baseline. The multitask-versus-single-task sentiment macro-F1 delta is only $+0.0042$ with 95\% confidence interval $[-0.0915, 0.0954]$ and $p=0.9510$, while multitask-versus-baseline sentiment macro-F1 is worse by $-0.1513$ with $p=0.0025$.

Read conservatively, these numbers support a \emph{solid within-study} authenticity gain but only a \emph{caution-level} sentiment interpretation. The authenticity effect is consistent across the current fixed test protocol, yet its external validity remains limited by domain and language confounding. The sentiment comparison is much less stable because its confidence interval is wide and the neutral class has only five test examples. In addition, the current pairwise significance tests should be treated as planned contrasts within one study protocol rather than as a fully multiplicity-controlled confirmatory analysis.

The confusion-matrix patterns reinforce this reading. On the representative pilot checkpoint, the sentiment-only Transformer recovers none of the five neutral test examples, whereas the baseline recovers three and the multitask model recovers two. The main sentiment weakness of the multitask model is a large number of positive-to-negative confusions, which explains why its aggregate sentiment result remains below the baseline despite being roughly tied with the sentiment-only Transformer in Macro-F1. For authenticity, the qualitative picture is more favorable: the multitask model misses only one fake review on the representative pilot checkpoint, while the authenticity-only Transformer overpredicts the fake class and produces many authentic-to-fake errors. These repository-side diagnostics are useful because they show that the pilot's main finding is not a metric artifact alone; it is also visible in the structure of model errors.

\subsection{Interpretation Against H1--H3}

The current study supports a more precise interpretation than a generic claim of ``mixed results.'' On the present protocol, \textbf{H1} is not supported: multitask sentiment does not improve meaningfully over the sentiment-only Transformer and remains clearly below the classical baseline. \textbf{H2} is supported within the current study scope: multitask training improves authenticity detection relative to the tested comparison models on the fixed mixed-source split. \textbf{H3} remains only partially resolved. The study shows that transfer can help one task without helping the other, but a stronger robustness claim still requires explicit source-, domain-, and language-sliced reporting rather than inference from pooled scores alone.

The repository package also includes an automatically generated article appendix that consolidates the pilot comparison table, paired statistical contrasts, representative confusion matrices, majority baselines, and the current expanded audit snapshot. This appendix is not itself additional experimental evidence, but it materially improves the reproducibility and inspectability of the study package that accompanies the manuscript.

\section{Threats to Validity and Limitations}

The study has several important limitations.

First, public authenticity datasets differ substantially in label semantics. \texttt{OpSpam} reflects a controlled deceptive-writing setup, \texttt{FraudDataset} relies on silver fraud signals, and \texttt{MAiDE-up} targets AI-generated reviews. These are related but not identical manifestations of authenticity.

Second, Russian-language e-commerce resources currently provide much stronger coverage for sentiment than for authenticity. This asymmetry creates the risk that one branch of the multitask model receives richer supervision than the other.

Third, harmonizing all authenticity signals into a single binary target (\texttt{authentic}/\texttt{fake}) inevitably simplifies meaningfully different phenomena. Final experiments should therefore include dataset-specific reporting and discussion of whether binary harmonization is empirically justified. If enough data are available, a multi-class or hierarchical authenticity formulation should also be considered as an ablation against the simpler binary setup.

Fourth, the current cross-domain setup is intrinsically confounded. The study mixes Russian e-commerce sentiment data with multilingual hospitality authenticity data, which means that the shared encoder may learn domain or language shortcuts instead of truly task-general features. This is precisely why broader claims must be checked with source-controlled and slice-based evaluation rather than pooled accuracy alone.

Fifth, the current transparency layer is not a fully causal interpretability framework. It improves practical transparency, but it does not solve interpretability in a strict theoretical sense.

Sixth, the current repository snapshot contains a completed low-resource comparison package, but not a multi-benchmark evaluation across all planned authenticity families. The present article should therefore be interpreted as a bounded empirical study rather than as an attempt to close the entire benchmark space at once.

Seventh, the executed study remains small: 2{,}000 total reviews, 200 sentiment test examples, 100 authenticity-labeled test examples, only three train seeds, and extremely sparse support for the neutral sentiment class in the test split. This is enough for a useful controlled comparison, but not enough for benchmark-level certainty.

Eighth, although the current article reports repeated runs, confidence intervals, significance-oriented comparisons, a task-ablation summary, and repository-side confusion-matrix diagnostics, broader generalization still requires dataset-specific authenticity reporting, larger data coverage, and source-controlled robustness checks.

Ninth, the larger audited \texttt{balanced6k} preparation snapshot is a protocol-strengthening artifact rather than completed model evidence. It confirms leakage-safe splitting on the current corpus preparation and improves visibility into label coverage, but its authenticity supervision still comes only from \texttt{MAiDE-up}, and its sentiment test split still contains only eight neutral examples. For that reason it should be cited as infrastructure maturity, not as a solved empirical benchmark.

Tenth, the work should not be interpreted as advocating fully autonomous moderation or evaluation. The current system can help structure experiments, surface uncertainties, and improve reproducibility, but it does not remove the need for human oversight in data curation, methodological decisions, or claim interpretation.

\section{Discussion}

The most scientifically interesting outcome of this line of work is not simply the best F1 score on a single dataset, but the behavior of transfer across tasks and domains. The current study already provides an informative answer: transfer is asymmetric. Authenticity benefits from shared training on the present low-resource mixed corpus, whereas sentiment does not show a meaningful gain over the sentiment-only Transformer and remains clearly below the classical baseline. This result is scientifically valuable in its own right because it shows that task relatedness in this problem is conditional rather than automatically beneficial.

From a systems perspective, a shared encoder also reduces inference complexity relative to two separate Transformer services. For an e-commerce deployment, that matters: lower operational cost, a single export format, simpler maintenance, and a cleaner integration path into moderation or analytics workflows.

The growing importance of AI-generated reviews further strengthens the relevance of the work. By 2026, authenticity detection should not be framed only as a problem of human-written deception; it must also address synthetic review generation and cross-generator generalization. This is one reason the combined perspective of the article matters: sentiment analysis by itself cannot distinguish trustworthy user feedback from persuasive but synthetic text.

At the same time, the article should resist the temptation to collapse all authenticity settings into one headline number. A broader follow-up study will need to separate at least three evaluation regimes: sentiment on e-commerce reviews, authenticity on AI-generated reviews, and mixed or cross-domain authenticity under heterogeneous label semantics.

That is also why the repository's newer audit and appendix layers matter. They do not magically resolve the scientific limitations of the present pilot, but they reduce ambiguity about what was executed, what was only prepared, and where the next robustness-oriented rerun should begin. For a manuscript in this stage, that kind of transparency is part of the quality of the contribution.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper presents a multitask Transformer-based model and a web system for joint sentiment analysis and review authenticity detection in e-commerce. Instead of treating the two tasks as separate services, the proposed approach uses a shared encoder with task-specific heads and supports partially labeled heterogeneous corpora.

The study provides a clear methodological scope, a unified data schema, a practical deployment architecture, and a completed comparative result package on real public data. It also clarifies how heterogeneous authenticity benchmarks can be incorporated into one supervised learning workflow without pretending that their labels are semantically identical. The central empirical finding is asymmetric transfer: under the executed low-resource mixed-source protocol, shared training improves authenticity detection but does not improve sentiment relative to the strongest available baseline.

The resulting contribution is therefore narrower than a benchmark paper, but also more defensible. Rather than claiming general multitask superiority, the paper shows that joint training for sentiment and authenticity is feasible under partial supervision, empirically useful for authenticity in the completed pilot, and best interpreted through explicit scope conditions. The surrounding repository package now also documents the study more rigorously through an audited expanded-corpus snapshot and an automatically consolidated results appendix. Broader claims about robustness, domain transfer, or universal gains remain future work and should be established only through larger, source-controlled evaluation.
